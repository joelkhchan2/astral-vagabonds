# Writing for Astral Vagabonds

Everything you write for this campaign gets read at a table or scanned mid-session by a busy DM. Write for that. Two standards apply to every piece of prose: sound like a person, and run like DM prep. This skill is the reference; `rules/DMing Craft.md` is the longer craft doc; `CLAUDE.md` carries the short version.

## Part 1 -- Kill the AI tells

The DM can spot machine-written prose and does not want it. These are the habits that give it away. Avoid all of them.

### The em-dash. Hard rule: do not use it.
No `--`, no `&mdash;`, no `—`. It is the single loudest tell. Rewrite every place you would reach for one:
- A hard break becomes a full stop. *"She teaches instead of ordering -- a closed door where her past should be"* becomes *"She teaches instead of ordering. Where her past should be, there is a closed door."*
- An aside becomes a comma pair or parentheses. *"Meech, forty years behind the grille, stamps the form."*
- A lead-in to a list or payoff becomes a colon. *"Three things are wrong tonight: the route ran long, the port has drifted, the runner never came back."*
Read the sentence back. If it only holds together with a dash, it is two sentences or it wants a comma. This is a house rule, not a suggestion.

### The "not X, but Y" reflex. Cut it.
The machine loves antithesis: *"not a monster, but a collector," "cut off, not killed," "it isn't about the money, it's about trust."* One of these in a whole document is fine for real emphasis. A pattern of them is the tell. Prefer the plain positive statement. Say what a thing *is* and stop. If the contrast genuinely matters, make it a full separate sentence, not a comma-hinge.

### The rule of three. Break it.
AI writing stacks everything in threes: three adjectives, three-item lists, three parallel clauses. Vary your counts. Use one strong adjective instead of three weak ones. Let a list be two items, or four, or a single word. If you catch three parallel phrases in a row, delete one or add one.

### Dead vocabulary. Banned or rationed.
Never: *delve, tapestry, testament to, a symphony of, a dance of, nestled, bustling, vibrant (as filler), rich (as filler), boasts, whimsical, navigate the complexities, at its core, in a world where, it's worth noting, ultimately, in summary, that said, whether X or Y* (as a sweeping frame), *from X to Y* (as a sweeping frame). These are chrome. Cut them and the sentence gets truer.

### Trailing "-ing" tails. Prune them.
The reflex to end a sentence with *"..., ensuring the crew stays fed," "..., allowing them to escape," "..., making it a memorable moment."* These smuggle in a conclusion the reader should draw. End on the concrete thing. Start a new sentence if the consequence matters.

### Hollow profundity and throat-clearing. Delete on sight.
No windup ("It's important to understand that..."), no summary sentence that restates the paragraph, no thesis-statement openers. A DM note earns attention by being useful in its first four words, not by framing itself.

### Over-balance. Loosen it.
Machine prose is suspiciously symmetrical: every sentence the same length, every idea paired. Write some short ones. Let one run long. Fragments are fine. A person's rhythm is uneven.

## Part 2 -- Write like DM prep, not an essay

### Read-aloud (boxed text) rules
This is what the DM says out loud to the players. It is a performance script, not a room description.
- **Short.** Two to four sentences, then stop and hand it back with "what do you do?" A wall of read-aloud loses the table.
- **Senses before sight, and pick two or three.** Lead with sound and smell where you can; they are the strongest second impression. Do not narrate all five senses.
- **Second person, present tense.** "You come up on the Rock from below. The gravity turns over as you berth."
- **Broad strokes, detail on demand.** A few vivid, concrete things that invite questions beat a paragraph that answers them all. Leave white space for the players to poke at.
- **One relatable anchor grounds the fantastic.** A dented mug, wet-rock smell, a bell somewhere. The ordinary detail is what makes the cosmic vista real.
- **Never bury a clue in prose.** If the players must notice something, it gets its own short beat, not a subordinate clause in sentence three.
- **End on a hook or an image**, especially for a cold open. Cut on the wrong detail, the thing unsaid, the question.
- **Say it out loud before you commit it.** Tongue-twisters, accidental alliteration, and unpronounceable names surface only when spoken. If you stumble, rewrite.

### DM notes rules
This is what the DM reads silently, at a glance, while running. Optimize for scanning.
- **Front-load the usable thing.** The trigger, the DC, the number, the reveal goes first, not after a sentence of setup. A DM's eye lands on the first few words.
- **Bullets and bold keys over paragraphs.** Prose is for read-aloud. Prep is for lists. Bold the word the DM is hunting for (the **DC**, the **tell**, the **name**).
- **Separate what players get from what the DM knows.** Never blur read-aloud, mechanics, and secret backstory into one block. Label them.
- **Secrets as a list of facts, not a story.** During play a DM needs "here are the five true things, reveal them in any order," not a narrative paragraph they have to reverse-engineer under pressure.
- **Give every NPC a line to actually say,** plus one voice tic, one physical tic, one want. A quoted line the DM can read beats three sentences describing how the NPC talks.
- **Options, not a script.** Present situations, stimuli, and spotlight openings. Especially for the two PCs (Orena, Gabrielle; Aerion, Priscilla), never write what they choose, buy, say, or feel. See the player-agency rule in `CLAUDE.md`.

### Tone compass (always)
70% found-family warmth, 20% cosmic wonder and eerie beauty, 10% real danger. PG to mild T. Land every eerie beat back in warmth. The comedy comes from the characters, never from the setting being a joke.

## Self-check before you finish
Run this pass on anything you write for the campaign:
1. Search the text for `--`, `—`, `&mdash;`. Zero allowed. Fix each.
2. Find every "not X, but/it's Y" hinge. Keep at most one, only if it truly earns it.
3. Count adjective and list triads. Break the pattern.
4. Scan for the dead-vocabulary list. Cut every hit.
5. Read every read-aloud block out loud. If you stumble or run past four sentences, cut.
6. Confirm no PC choice was scripted.
