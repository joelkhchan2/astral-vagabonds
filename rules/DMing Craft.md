# DMing Craft -- narration, scene-setting, and running the table

A working reference for running *Astral Vagabonds*, distilled from published GM advice (sources at the bottom) and tuned to this campaign's tone. The one-paragraph version lives in `CLAUDE.md`; this is the fuller guide. None of it is a rule; all of it is a habit worth building.

## Scene-setting and description

**Lead with two or three senses, and not the obvious one.** Sight is the default and the weakest opener because everyone reaches for it. Sound and smell are the strongest *second* impressions and the fastest way to make a place feel real -- the tick of a cooling engine, wet rock and cooking, the specific quiet of a room where something died. Aim for at least three senses in any location you want the table to remember. (DMDavid; The Gamer.)

**Broad strokes first, detail on demand.** Paint the room in two or three vivid sentences, then stop and ask "what do you do?" Players will pull on the threads they care about, and you fill in specifics as they ask. A short description that starts a conversation beats a long one that ends it. (DMDavid; Alpine DM.)

**Ground the fantastic in the ordinary.** A cosmic vista lands *through* a relatable detail: the flurries of soot in the air of an iron fortress, the flexing gantry under a mile-wide asteroid, the dented mug someone grabbed before the railing. One concrete, mundane anchor makes the strange believable. Specific always beats grand. (DMDavid.)

**Say the character's name and put them in it.** "Orena, you find the port by feel, not by the numbers" is sharper than "the ship arrives." Address players by their character's name and give each their own sensory line when you can.

**Hand narration back.** When a player succeeds at something cool, ask *them* how they did it and let them describe it. Shared narration costs you nothing and makes the win theirs. (DMDavid.)

## Running the session

**Open on a strong start.** Begin each session (and most scenes) at the interesting moment -- an image, an action, a question -- not the logistics that precede it. A strong start is often, but not always, a fight; a star going out on watch is a strong finish. (Sly Flourish, *Return of the Lazy Dungeon Master*.)

**Prep secrets and clues, not scripts.** Keep a short list of loose facts the players might discover -- each a tweet-sized, useful thing -- untied to any specific room or NPC, and reveal them wherever the players actually go. This is how you seed the Guttering: symptoms as mild unease, dealt like ordinary gossip, assembling into a pattern the table notices on its own. Never name the mystery for them. (Sly Flourish.)

**Think in scenes, roughly one per 45 minutes.** A session is a handful of scenes plus the glue between them. Know the two or three that matter (here: the arrival, the cold open) and protect their time; let the rest breathe or be cut. (Sly Flourish.)

**A few fantastic locations beat many bland ones.** One place with a real secret, a real danger, and a real opportunity is worth five rooms of scenery. That is exactly how the location docs in this repo are built -- honor the format when you improvise.

**Pace by contrast.** Follow a loud beat with a quiet one, a haggle with a vista, a laugh with a chill. The campaign's descending emotional shapes only work if the highs are genuinely warm first.

## NPCs and voices

**One voice tic, one physical tic, one want.** That is the whole kit for a memorable NPC: a way they sound (Meech explains the correct form; Ottie trades in candy), a thing they do with their body or hands (Estra's mug, Tack patting a bulkhead), and something they want in the scene. You can play anyone again with those three.

**Let NPCs be competent and human, not exposition.** Even a one-scene factor has a reason they're squeezing the colony. Complexity over alignment: people act from curiosity, fear, survival, or ambition. Villains are rare here; problems are the antagonist.

**The crew have voices already.** Use `npcs/Crew Comedy Bits.md` -- the running gags and quiet moments are pre-written characterization you can drop in to make the ship feel lived-in between plot beats.

## Combat, run warm

**Frame the fight, then let the dice talk.** Two sentences of what it looks and sounds like, then initiative. Come back to the fiction on big hits and misses -- describe the near-thing, the crew station under fire -- so it's a scene, not a spreadsheet.

**Pre-roll enemy attacks when you want speed.** Rolling a few rounds of the enemy's to-hit and damage ahead of time (see the encounter tables in the session companions) keeps a low-stakes fight moving and lets you stay in the fiction instead of the math. Ignore a pre-roll the moment the story wants something else.

**Danger is usually expensive, not lethal.** Combat frightens because it costs -- hull at 20 gp/HP, a loose end, a body count they chose. Let the players decide whether to press or let a beaten enemy run; that choice tells you who this crew is.

## The table itself

**Say yes, or roll for it.** A good idea from a player beats a good idea from you. When they try something clever the rules don't cover, set a DC and let them.

**Leave silence after the cold open.** Do not explain, do not cut away, do not offer a check. Let the image sit, then end the session. The dread is one held moment; talking over it kills it.

**Keep the guardrail.** PG to mild T, uncanny not gross, and every eerie beat lands back in the warmth of the crew (`campaign/Story Spine.md`). When in doubt, dial toward wonder and come home to found family.
