# Downtime Games (gambling and bar play)

Simple, fast games to run at a table when the crew has money to lose and time to kill. Both PCs enjoy this, so keep the mechanics light and the stakes small (1 to 10 gp a hand fits the money reality; a big night is 50). Use them at the **Compass Rose** on Bral and the **Long Shadow** at Hallow's Rest, or anywhere people drink. They are a low-stakes way to hand the players a scene with an NPC who won't sell information but might lose it across a table.

**How to run a hand at the real table:** actually roll the dice with the players. Let them decide when to hold or bluff. Resolve a whole hand in two minutes. The point is the banter and the read on the NPC, not the gold.

---

## Scavver (dice, closest to twenty)

The house game. Fast, intuitive for anyone who knows blackjack, and thematically "don't get eaten."

**Play.** Everyone antes to the pot. On your turn you roll a **d6** at a time and add it to your running total, going as close to **20** as you dare. Stop whenever you like and "hold." Go over 20 and **the scavver gets you**: you bust and you're out of the hand. When everyone has held or busted, the highest total that is 20 or under **takes the pot**. Ties split it.

**The house.** If an NPC runs the table (Big Pol, a Compass Rose regular), the house plays last and **holds on 16 or higher**, so the players can read the odds. On a tie with the house, the house pays even money.

**Nerve, not skill.** No check is needed to play; it's push-your-luck. If a player wants an *edge*, that's cheating (below).

**Flavor.** "Sitting on seventeen" is a real decision. Big Pol narrates every roll like a hunt: *"eighteen, and here comes the dark..."*

---

## Liar's Berth (bluffing dice)

The social game, best with three or more, and the one where information leaks. A liar's-dice variant.

**Play.** Each player has **three d6** rolled in secret under a cup. On your turn you make a **claim** about how many of a given face are on the *whole* table, counting everyone's hidden dice (for example, "five threes"). Each claim must raise the last, either a higher quantity or the same quantity of a higher face. Ones are wild and count as any face.

On your turn you may instead **call "Berth"** (call the last claimer a liar). Everyone reveals. If the claim was true (there really are that many or more), the caller loses a die. If it was false, the claimer loses a die. Lose all three dice and you're out; last player holding dice wins the pot.

**The read.** This is a bluffing game, so play it as one: the tell is the NPC, not a roll. A nervous Warden over-claims when she's holding nothing. Bex Halloway always calls one round too early. Let the players learn the person by playing the game.

**Cheating (either game).** A player who wants to palm a die, miscount, or signal a partner rolls **Dexterity (Sleight of Hand)** against the sharpest opponent's **passive Perception** (Ulve's is very high; a drunk Warden's is not). Success shifts one die or one count their way. Failure is a scene: at the Rose, Ulve simply stops serving you and it costs a favour to fix; at a rough table, it's the start of a brawl. Catching *someone else* cheating is the same check in reverse and a fine social lever.

## Stakes and story hooks

- **Keep it small.** These people are broke. A 10 gp pot is a real one. Losing a night's winnings should sting and never matter.
- **Play for information, not gold.** The best use of a game is a side bet: *"I'll tell you what really impounded the* Steadfast Promise *if you can beat me at Scavver."* Warden Cathis, Dov Errin, and Bex Halloway all say things across a dice table they would never say sober and standing.
- **Character in the play.** Finny runs the bracket and loses cheerfully. Oz is politely, alarmingly good and gives his winnings back. Brass never loses and nobody can prove why. Estra has, inexplicably, never lost a hand of anything, and won't say how.
