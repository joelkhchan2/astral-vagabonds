# Episode -- The Ambassadors (drop-in comedy episode)

> **A one-shot / half-session you can drop between missions.** Not on the contract spine. First contact, *inverted*: the crew are the strange visitors from the sky, and the people they meet are terrified of them. Plays as **comedy** -- culture-clash, mistaken intentions, the crew being far more alarming than they mean to be -- with a warm landing and, if you want it, one quiet arc thread underneath. Tone: PG, the lightest thing in the campaign. A palate-cleanser after something heavy.

## The pitch

The crew makes planetfall (or docks at an isolated habitat / generation ship / sky-island) somewhere so far off the lanes that **the locals have never seen a spelljammer, and don't believe in them.** To these people, a ship descending out of the black sky crewed by a water genasi, an astral elf, a plasmoid, a talking-to-the-hull engineer, and *a small dragon* is not "a trade vessel." It is an omen. The crew are the aliens. They are here to buy fresh water and offload cargo; the locals are fairly sure they are here to end the world.

**Why they're stopping:** pick the least dramatic reason -- topping off water and air, a repair that needs a gravity well, waiting out a hazard, a cargo drop the manifest insists exists here. The comedy engine is that the crew's stakes are *tiny and mundane* ("we just need forty barrels of water and we'll go") while the locals' stakes are *cosmic and total* ("the Sky People have come, as the old warnings said"). Neither side can hear the other over their own frame.

## Who the locals are (pick one)

- **A lost colony** that arrived generations ago, forgot it came from the stars, and now has a mythology in which the sky is a lid and things beyond it are the end of the world. (Best for pathos under the comedy.)
- **A generation ship** decades into a voyage, whose people have never docked with anyone and whose entire culture is the ship's rules. (Tables.md #17.)
- **A pre-spaceflight world** in a quiet system, with just enough magic or myth that "visitors from the sky" slots into an existing prophecy -- usually a dire one.
- **A sky-island / habitat** cut off so long its people assume they're the last ones alive.

Whichever you pick, the load-bearing fact is: **they are not stupid, they are not primitive, they are simply working from a completely different map, and by their map the crew is a catastrophe.** Play them with dignity. The joke is the *mismatch*, never that the locals are dumb.

## The comedy engine (how to run the mismatch)

Every crew member's ordinary behavior reads to the locals as a portent. Lean into it:

- **The astral elf (Aerion) doesn't age and speaks too well** -- clearly the deathless herald from the warnings. He's just trying to negotiate a water price. His perfect manners make it *worse*.
- **The plasmoid (Brass) is a person made of colour and hunger** -- the locals have a very specific and very wrong idea about what she eats. She is offended. She is also, unhelpfully, hungry.
- **Tack talks to the ship and the ship talks back** -- to the locals this is a person conversing with a god, or a demon, or the sky itself. Tack finds the whole thing very normal and cannot understand the fuss.
- **Flurry.** A dragon. It's a *baby* and it's *adorable* and to the locals it is the beast from the worst page of the book. Flurry, sensing fear, does something either terrifying or unbearably cute at the exact wrong moment.
- **Oz keeps trying to reassure people** in his big, sincere, heavily-armed way, which is the single most frightening thing that has happened to this settlement in a century.
- **Finny immediately makes one local best friend** (a kid, a fellow gossip, the village skeptic) who becomes the crew's only translator across the frame gap.

**Orena** and the water are your emotional throughline: a water genasi is, to a people who fear the sky, either the most reassuring thing they've met (something *of their world*, water, kindness) or the confirmation that the invaders can command the elements. Let Gabrielle play which. She's the crew's best bridge because she *listens* -- the navigator's habit of reading what's actually in front of her, applied to people.

## The shape (beats)

1. **The bad first impression.** The descent, the panic, an honest misunderstanding that makes everything worse (a warning shot, a barred gate, a "sacrifice" the crew mistakes for a gift, a gift the crew mistakes for a threat). Establish: everyone is acting reasonably by their own map, and the maps do not touch.
2. **The escalating farce.** Attempts to fix it dig the hole deeper. The crew's competence is *useless* here because competence assumes a shared frame and there isn't one. This is the fun. Every solution creates a fresh omen.
3. **The bridge.** Someone -- usually Orena or Finny's new local friend -- finds the one true thing that crosses the gap. Not a speech; a *small shared act*: fixing something the settlement needed, sitting through a meal, saving a kid, a piece of music (Orena is a bard -- a song can do what words can't, and it's earned, not a button). The frame cracks open.
4. **The reframe.** The locals see the crew as people; the crew see the locals as people. The "prophecy" gets gently, humanly reinterpreted -- not debunked in a way that shames anyone, but *widened* so both sides fit inside it. Warmth lands.
5. **The departure.** They got their water. They leave a settlement slightly changed and a little less alone, and they take a new friend-of-the-ship, a strange local custom Finny will now enforce forever, and a story they'll tell at dinner. Back to the black.

## The optional arc thread (drop it if you want pure fluff)

*Why* is this place so isolated? The load-bearing answer, if you want one: **their route went dark.** A generation or two ago ships still came; then the road stopped working, the visitors stopped arriving, and the settlement's fear of the sky *dates from exactly then* -- the year the sky "abandoned" them. They don't know the word Guttering; they built a religion around the wound. If the crew looks, the timeline lines up with the other dark-route symptoms, and the local "end times began when the star-people stopped coming" is one more data point on Aerion's quiet pattern. Play it as a chill under the comedy, one line, not a lecture: *these people aren't superstitious, they're survivors of a symptom nobody's named yet.* (Ties to `campaign/Story Spine.md` and the Tier 1 pattern in `campaign/Tier 1.md`.)

It also seeds a **re-contact hook**: the crew now knows a cut-off world that fresh, living-sky navigation could reconnect. When the nomadic method works in a later tier, *these* are people it visibly saves. A face for the cure.

## Both PCs lead

- **Aerion:** the negotiation and the logistics of trading with a people who have no shared currency, no shared law, and a religious objection to his existence. A pure Aerion problem -- solved with patience, a ledger, and reading what the settlement actually values. His deathlessness being *the exact thing they fear* gives Priscilla a real character beat about how his difference reads to strangers.
- **Orena:** first contact as *listening*. She's the bridge -- the water, the song, the navigator's eye for what's really there. If Gabrielle wants a lighter combat load, this is the episode for it; the "fight" here is entirely social, and Orena's the one who wins it.

## Tone guardrail

PG, and the warmest, silliest thing in the campaign. Nobody is a monster; the locals are sympathetic and the crew are lovable even while being accidentally terrifying. **No one gets hurt, no settlement gets wrecked.** The danger, such as it is, is a misunderstanding spiralling -- and it resolves in understanding and a meal. If a moment gets genuinely tense, that's your cue to cut to Flurry doing something ridiculous. Land it on the crew being, once again, a found family that leaves people better than it found them.
