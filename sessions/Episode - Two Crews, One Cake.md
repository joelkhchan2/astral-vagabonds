# Episode -- Two Crews, One Cake (drop-in, from the entertainer posting)

> **A light, standalone palate-cleanser.** Not story-bearing, not on any spine, no complication that needs to matter past this session. Pure sitcom rhythm: setup, escalation, a punchline that turns out to have a little warmth under it. Tone: PG, silly, competitive without ever turning mean. Good drop-in whenever the table wants a session that's all found-family comedy and zero stakes.

## The posting

*"Wanted: lively entertainer(s) for a children's birthday, games and tricks preferred, pay generous, please arrive in costume."* Reads exactly like what it says. Mostly is. **Qualifier:** none, and that's the joke, any crew desperate or bored enough to take a kids' party gig is exactly who this posting is fishing for.

**Play the crew's own reluctance up front.** This is a step down from boarding hostile hulls and everyone at the table should be allowed to say so out loud. That reluctance is the setup the whole episode pays off.

## The clients

**Corvin Ashby and Mireille Talvane**, captains of two small trading outfits, and old rivals over a soured deal years back that neither will fully explain. **Their kids, Birdie and Milo, are best friends anyway**, birthdays four days apart, and this year the two of them insisted on one joint party rather than two separate ones. Neither parent trusted the other to actually handle the fun half properly, so each quietly hired their own entertainment without telling the other.

**Corvin's tell:** talks about his half of the party in logistics terms, headcounts and timing, and gets visibly rattled the moment anything isn't going to plan. **Mireille's tell:** relentlessly gracious in public, sharper in an aside the second nobody important is listening. Neither is a villain, this is two proud people who love their kids and can't stand each other, playing nice in the same backyard for an afternoon.

## The rival: Marchetta Vale and the Lucky Star Revue

A polished, professional troupe on the party circuit: a bard running rehearsed illusion tricks, acrobats, a puppeteer, matching costumes, a routine tight enough to run identically at every stop. **Voice tic:** performs warmth at adults the same way she performs it at children, projected, theatrical, "and wouldn't you all just *love* to see something wonderful." **Physical tic:** resets her cuffs and checks her reflection in anything shiny between bits, never lets a seam show. **Want:** to stay the obvious, uncontested choice on this circuit, and never share a booking again.

**She meets the crew assuming they're the backup**, in case a trick goes wrong, and says so, lightly, cheerfully, devastatingly. That's the spark. Nobody on this crew wants to be told they're the backup act at a birthday party by someone who's never boarded a hostile hull in her life, and the reluctant grumbling flips to real investment the instant she says it.

## The shape of the scene

1. **Arrival, and the reveal.** The crew shows up to set up and finds the Lucky Star Revue already there, mid-unload, equally confused. Both parents, cornered in front of their own guests, pretend this was always the plan rather than admit the mistake. Let the table enjoy the confusion before anyone commits to a side.
2. **The condescension.** Marchetta places the crew as amateur muscle within the first minute of meeting them. This is the turn, played for a real, satisfying sting, not just comedy, before it curdles into competitive resolve.
3. **The games, escalating.** Whatever party games are on the sheet (a zero-g egg-and-spoon race, pin the tail on the void whale, a magic-trick showdown) become the battlefield, both crews topping each other bit by bit. Give every NPC a real beat: Oz consciously holding back so he doesn't actually win by force, Finny cheating and getting caught in the most Finny way possible, Brass patching a scraped knee with real medic hands instead of a prop bandage, Tack narrating the whole afternoon like a ship's log because that's simply who he is. Let this run as pure sitcom escalation, setup, one-upmanship, punchline, repeat.
4. **The kids catch on.** Partway through, Birdie and Milo clock that this has stopped being about them and started being a proxy war between their parents (and, by extension, between two entertainer crews). Give them a real moment to call it, gently or gleefully, their choice, not scripted for them, they're NPCs and can be played however lands best at the table.
5. **The turn.** Once the kids are in on it, the day tips from farce into the actual best birthday either of them has ever had, precisely because of the sheer, ridiculous excess of two crews going all out. Let Marchetta lose to something she genuinely can't rehearse: unforced, unrehearsed warmth. She notices. She doesn't enjoy it.

## Where it lands

**Not a clean truce.** Corvin and Mireille don't need to resolve their old grudge in one afternoon, and shouldn't, that's a bigger and different story than this one. What they get is a good day for their kids despite themselves, and maybe, if the table wants it, one small unscripted moment where the rivalry cracks just enough to be survivable next time the two families are in the same room.

**Marchetta remembers losing**, specifically, to something polish can't buy. She's a good, light recurring rival for the same lane of odd little jobs BoyBoy and Verity Gilt already occupy, sharper and better prepared at her next booking, still completely certain she should have won.
